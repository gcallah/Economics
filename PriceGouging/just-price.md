Just Price


What Aristotle says is, that in an exchange between for instance a shoemaker and
a farmer, "Let the farmer be a, his food y, the shoemaker beta, the work of his
that is being equalized [to y], delta; if it were not possible to have
reciprocity in this way, then there would be no community." Nichomachean Ethics,
V.5

"In itself [a transaction] should equally benefit both parties, so should be
based on an equality of material exchange" Summa Theologiae, II.11.77



Carl Menger argues against this Aristotelian view as follows:

"the... error of regarding the quantities of goods in an exchange as
equivalents. The result was incalculable damage to our science since writers in
the field of price theory lost themselves in attempts to solve the problem of
discovering the causes of an alleged equality between two quantities of goods

(principles of economics, the theory of Price, 192)

And Menger attributes this error to Aristotle in particular, when he writes "The
error of regarding the quantities of goods in exchange as equivalents was made
as early as Aristotle..." (principles, appendix F, 305)

If Menger has properly understood Aristotle, then he is certainly correct that
the philosopher was mistaken. As I explained in Economics for Real People:

"If two people exchange when they consider the value of what they are getting to
be equal to the value of what they are giv- ing up, there is no reason that they
shouldn’t simply reverse the trade a moment later. If you sell your house for
$200,000, then you valued $200,000 more highly than you did your house.
Conversely, the buyer valued your house more highly than he did $200,000.
Otherwise (ignoring transaction costs), there is no reason that, as soon as the
exchange is made, you wouldn’t immediately take the house back and give up the
$200,000. In fact, if the exchange took place at a point of equal valuation,
there is no reason you and the other party shouldn’t swap the house back and
forth any number of times."

But I believe a closer look at Aristotle's text suggests that he meant something
different than what Menger thought he meant. A key passage supporting my belief
is the following:








Let us say that I'm selling a rocking chair at a flea market, and a buyer makes
me an offer. The buyer would pay up to $12 for the chair, while I would accept
as little as eight dollars. So here, the just price is $10.

But I might notice that the potential buyer is a mother, with three young kids
in tell who does not look to be particularly well off, judging by the state of
her and her kids clothes. Once I see that, as an act of charity, I might say
"no, that's too much: just give me six dollars." That, of course, is morally
praiseworthy. But it is going above and beyond the Jess price: everyone should
be encouraged to perform acts of charity, but everyone has some limit as to how
many such acts they can perform. We cannot expect everyone to engage in acts of
charity in every interaction they have throughout their entire lives.

This understanding offers us an insight into the pricing situation of an area
that is just suffered in natural catastrophe. Increased prices for vital goods
in such an area are a matter of great contention.

So let us look at how this idea would apply to an area that just suffered from a
terrible hurricane. The people there are in dire need of drinkable water, food
staples, materials for rebuilding their houses, and more.

In response, many suppliers send those goods to that area while charging a
higher price than they do in other places. This leads to complaints about "price
gouging": these businesses are taking advantage of people's distress to earn
high profits.

In response, defenders of free markets often point out that is the higher price
that, in fact, motivates suppliers to redirect goods to the disaster-stricken
area. If we look at the idea of a bargaining range, and follow the suggestion
here that the just price is roughly in the middle of it, then we can acknowledge
some truth in both of the above positions.

It is certainly not right to take undue advantage of people who are already
suffering. At the same time, businesses exist to make a profit: they are not the
Red Cross or Habitat for Humanity. The doctrine of just price suggests that the
price should be set where both parties are happy with the deal. The victims of
the disaster should understand that it is perfectly reasonable, and, in fact,
their best chance of getting the goods they need, to pay a somewhat higher price
than normal in the wake of a disaster. Businesses, on the other hand, should not
try to squeeze every penny they can out of those victims. The basic idea would
be captured in the phrase, "let's split the difference."

So, if a lumber company would be willing to supply plywood to the affected area
at a minimum price of $1 per square foot, while those in the affected area would
be willing to pay up to $2 per square foot, then we might say that the just
price is roughly $1.50.

This is not an idea that will yield exact numerical answers! We are engaged in
moral reasoning, not in mathematical calculation. The basic idea is that both
parties should walk away feeling that they have each gained roughly the same
amount from the transaction.

Furthermore, if my understanding of just price in these situations is accepted,
it does not therefore guild any pat answer as to what the authorities ought to
do in such a case. The authorities might always be tempted to play the populist
card, and legally required that no price increase is permissible when selling
goods in the disaster area. But that approach lacks prudence: it will, with very
high probability, reduce the quality of needed goods sent into the disaster
area. The government, might instead, set some percentage limit on the amount a
price may increase in the emergency zone. But given the difficulty that
officials will always have in determining exactly what the minimum price the
seller would accept and the maximum price that a buyer would pay, such an
attempt faces a serious knowledge problem in determining where to set that
percentage.

In any case, this is not an essay about optimal policy, and I do not mean to
recommend any particular policy in such situations. Rather, my only aim has been
to show that there is a perfectly sensible meaning we can ascribe (?) to the
notion of a just price, and, this meaning is perfectly compatible with modern
economic theory.
